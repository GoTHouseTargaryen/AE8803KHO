\section{Approach}

\subsection{Time-Expanded Network and DP Formulation}

The assembly campaign is modeled as a finite-horizon Markov
decision process over a time-expanded network. Decision epochs are
discretized into configurable periods of length $\Delta t$
(default: 7 days). The system state at epoch $t$ is

\begin{equation}
  S(t) = \bigl\{\mathcal{M}(t),\; \mathcal{C}(t),\;
                \mathcal{Q}(t),\; n_{\mathrm{tugs}}(t)\bigr\}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathcal{M}(t)$ is the set of completed module
identifiers, tracking cumulative build progress as a monotonically
growing set; $\mathcal{C}(t)$ is the set of docked crew vehicles
together with their remaining rotation periods, enabling the model
to anticipate crew expiration and schedule rotations before assembly
stalls; $\mathcal{Q}(t)$ is the ordered queue of cargo in transit,
each entry carrying the module manifest and the scheduled arrival
period so the optimizer can pipeline future launches without waiting
for each flight to land; and $n_{\mathrm{tugs}}(t)$ counts reusable
transfer stages currently available at LEO for reassignment to the
next cargo flight.
Together these four quantities constitute the minimal information
needed to evaluate all future decisions without referencing
campaign history.

At each epoch the optimizer selects a decision
$D(t) = \{\text{launches},\;\text{crew allocation},\;
\text{assembly tasks},\;\text{tug assignments}\}$
that transitions the system to state $S'$. The Bellman optimality
equation is

\begin{equation}
  V(S,t) = \min_{D \in \mathcal{D}(S,t)}
            \bigl[\, c(S, D, t) + V(S', t+1) \,\bigr]
\end{equation}

\noindent where $V(S,t)$ is the minimum achievable future cost
(cost-to-go) from state $S$ at epoch $t$ under all subsequent
optimal decisions; $c(S,D,t)$ is the immediate one-period cost of
taking decision $D$, which aggregates launch costs, first-flight
premiums, and fractional weighted-objective penalties; and
$\mathcal{D}(S,t)$ is the feasible decision set enforced by
lead-time gates, pad-turnaround constraints, and DAG precedence
rules.
The Bellman recursion guarantees global optimality over the finite
horizon, but its state space---exponential in $|\mathcal{M}|$ due
to the module-set bitmask---is intractable beyond approximately
20~modules, motivating the beam-search approximation in
Section~\ref{sec:beam}.

\subsection{Multi-Objective Cost Function}
\label{sec:cost}

Three objectives are minimized simultaneously via a weighted sum
with safe normalization:

\begin{equation}
  J = w_1 \frac{N_{\mathrm{launches}}}{N_{\mathrm{launches}}^{\max}}
    + w_2 \frac{T}{T^{\max}}
    + w_3 \frac{C}{C^{\max}}
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $N_{\mathrm{launches}}$ is the cumulative number of
cargo and crew flights dispatched up to the current period;
$T$ is the number of scheduling periods elapsed;
$C$ is the total program cost in millions of dollars, including all
launch fees and first-flight premiums;
and $w_1, w_2, w_3 \geq 0$ are user-adjustable objective weights
set via sliders in the web interface.
A higher weight on any term directs the optimizer to minimize that
objective more aggressively: $w_1 \gg w_2, w_3$ produces the
fewest flights at the expense of a longer, more expensive
campaign; $w_2$ dominant front-loads cargo and shrinks transit
gaps; $w_3$ dominant substitutes cheap vehicles and delays the
more expensive ones.
Dividing each term by its upper bound converts all three to the
unit interval $[0,1]$, preventing any one objective from dominating
due to unit or magnitude differences---without normalization, raw
dollar cost would exceed the raw launch count by three to four
orders of magnitude.

The upper bound on launch count is
$N_{\mathrm{launches}}^{\max} = 2|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}|$,
which assumes at most two flights per module---a conservative
ceiling for mixed-capacity fleets.
The time bound is $T^{\max} = T_{\mathrm{horizon}}$
(default 200 periods, i.e.\ $\approx 3.8$ years), the maximum
number of scheduling epochs the solver is allowed to run.
The cost bound is set adaptively:

\begin{equation}
  C^{\max} = 2\,|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}| \cdot \bar{c}
  \label{eq:cmax}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\bar{c}$ is the \emph{median} per-launch cost
(including first-flight premium, Eq.~\ref{eq:firstflight}) across
all selected cargo and crew vehicles.
$|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}|$ is the total number of modules to
be assembled, and the factor of 2 mirrors the launch-count bound.
A static ceiling such as $\$2{,}000|\mathcal{M}|$\,M
would compress cheap-vehicle missions (e.g.\ Starship at \$100\,M)
to less than 5\% of the range, making the cost weight $w_3$
effectively irrelevant.
The median-based bound tracks the selected vehicle set and keeps
$C/C^{\max}$ in the same 30--80\% range as the launch and time
terms, ensuring that all three weights shift the optimizer
proportionally.
Safe normalization returns 0 when the upper bound is zero.

\subsection{Transfer Model}

Payload mass delivered to the L4 assembly site is computed via the
Tsiolkovsky rocket equation applied to the LEO-to-L4 transfer:

\begin{equation}
  m_{\mathrm{delivered}} = m_{\mathrm{LEO}}
    \cdot \exp\!\left(\frac{-\Delta v}{I_{sp}\, g_0}\right)
  \label{eq:rocket}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $m_{\mathrm{LEO}}$ is the vehicle's nominal payload
capacity to low Earth orbit;
$\Delta v$ is the velocity increment required for the chosen transfer
trajectory;
$I_{sp}$ is the specific impulse of the transfer stage propellant
(in seconds);
and $g_0 = 9.807 \times 10^{-3}$\,km/s$^2$ converts $I_{sp}$ to
consistent velocity units.
This equation quantifies the propulsive cost of moving mass from
LEO to L4: higher $\Delta v$ or lower $I_{sp}$ reduces deliverable
mass exponentially.
Two transfer trajectories are modeled: a direct Hohmann-like arc
($\Delta v = 4.1$\,km/s, 60-day transit) and a low-energy
weak-stability-boundary (WSB) trajectory ($\Delta v = 3.8$\,km/s,
120-day transit)~\cite{belbruno_wbs}.
The WSB route saves propellant at the cost of doubling transit time;
when the time weight $w_2$ dominates, the solver prefers the
Hohmann arc despite its higher propellant consumption.

Vehicles classified as L4-direct (Starship, SLS Block~2) apply
Eq.~(\ref{eq:rocket}) to their full LEO payload.
For LEO-only vehicles that require a separate transfer stage of
dry mass $m_d$ and propellant mass $m_p$, the tug mass ratio
$R = \exp(\Delta v / I_{sp} g_0)$ constrains the maximum cargo the
stage can push to L4:

\begin{align}
  m_{\mathrm{cargo,max}} &= \frac{R\,m_d - (m_d + m_p)}{1 - R}
  \label{eq:tug} \\
  m_{\mathrm{delivered}} &= \min\!\left(m_{\mathrm{payload}},\;
                               m_{\mathrm{cargo,max}}\right)
\end{align}

\noindent $m_{\mathrm{cargo,max}}$ is the maximum payload the tug
can accelerate to the required $\Delta v$ given its propellant budget;
$m_{\mathrm{payload}}$ is the cargo mass loaded onto the tug at LEO.
When the loaded payload exceeds this ceiling, deliverable mass is
capped, protecting the tug's $\Delta v$ budget.
This constraint explains why capable LEO-class vehicles such as
New Glenn (45\,t to LEO) may deliver only $\approx 9$\,t to L4
when paired with a chemical kick stage: the kick stage's limited
propellant and modest $I_{sp}$ impose a hard mass ceiling.

\subsection{Crew Model}

All crew aboard every docked vehicle are treated as a single
shared labor pool:

\begin{equation}
  n_{\mathrm{crew}} = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}(t)} n_v
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n_v$ is the number of crew members aboard vehicle
$v$ and the sum runs over all currently docked crew vehicles
$\mathcal{C}(t)$.
Pooling reflects the mission concept in which crew from different
vehicles can cross-operate at the assembly site; the total
$n_{\mathrm{crew}}$ directly determines the EVA and IVA allocation
each period.

EVA operations require a buddy system (pairs of astronauts working
together outside) and dedicated intravehicular activity (IVA)
monitors who remain inside to support and oversee EVA operations.
The stable allocation is solved iteratively:

\begin{equation}
  n_{\mathrm{IVA}} = \left\lceil
    \frac{n_{\mathrm{EVA\,pairs}}}{k_{\mathrm{IVA}}}
  \right\rceil, \quad
  n_{\mathrm{EVA\,pairs}} = \left\lfloor
    \frac{n_{\mathrm{crew}} - n_{\mathrm{IVA}}}{2}
  \right\rfloor
  \label{eq:eva}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $n_{\mathrm{IVA}}$ is the number of crew assigned as
IVA safety monitors; $n_{\mathrm{EVA\,pairs}}$ is the number of
two-person EVA teams; and $k_{\mathrm{IVA}}$ is the maximum number
of EVA pairs a single IVA monitor can safely oversee (default: 2).
The two equations must be satisfied simultaneously: more EVA pairs
require more IVA monitors, which in turn reduces the pool available
for EVA.
The fixed-point iteration converges in two to three steps for
crew sizes up to about 14.

Productive assembly hours generated by the EVA crew per period are:

\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm{EVA}} = n_{\mathrm{EVA\,pairs}} \cdot h_{\mathrm{session}}
                     \cdot d_{\mathrm{period}} \cdot \eta_d
  \label{eq:crew_hours}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $h_{\mathrm{session}}$ is the maximum EVA session
length per pair per day (default: 6\,h), $d_{\mathrm{period}}$ is
the number of operational days in a scheduling period (default: 7),
and $\eta_d = 0.6$ is a duty-cycle factor that reduces theoretical
EVA time to account for sleep, exercise, meals, suit maintenance,
and contingency task time.
Effective EVA hours per period therefore never exceed
$n_{\mathrm{EVA\,pairs}} \times 6 \times 7 \times 0.6 =
25.2 \times n_{\mathrm{EVA\,pairs}}$\,h, regardless of crew size.

Robotic arm stations operate continuously (24\,h/day) but at a
time-penalty $\eta_r = 1.5$ relative to crew EVA, reflecting the
longer setup, repositioning, and verification cycles needed for
teleoperated assembly:

\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm{robotic}} = \frac{n_{\mathrm{arms}} \cdot h_{\mathrm{period}}}{\eta_r}
\end{equation}

\noindent $n_{\mathrm{arms}}$ is the number of robotic arm stations
assembled and operational at the site (grows as robotic-arm modules
are completed); $h_{\mathrm{period}} = d_{\mathrm{period}} \times 24$
is the total hours in a period.
Each arm therefore contributes $7 \times 24 / 1.5 = 112$\,h of
equivalent assembly capacity per period---substantially less than
a full-time human pair but available without crew overhead or
duty-cycle losses.

Each crew vehicle also carries cargo mass in the space not occupied
by crew:

\begin{equation}
  m_{\mathrm{cargo}} = m_{\mathrm{capacity}}
                       - n_{\mathrm{crew}} \cdot m_{\mathrm{per\,crew}}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $m_{\mathrm{capacity}}$ is the vehicle's total
pressurized mass capacity; $m_{\mathrm{per\,crew}} = 200$\,kg
covers the seat, suit, life-support consumables, and personal
equipment for each crew member.
For large crewed vehicles such as Starship HLS ($m_{\mathrm{capacity}}
\gg n_{\mathrm{crew}} \times 200$\,kg), the residual cargo margin
is substantial and can carry spare parts or supplementary equipment
alongside crew.

\subsection{Proximity Operations and Congestion Model}

Simultaneous vehicle presence at the assembly site increases
coordination overhead, reduces usable docking ports, and elevates
collision risk---all of which reduce effective assembly throughput.
A scale-adaptive penalty function is applied to available crew
and robotic hours each period:

\begin{equation}
  \pi(n,\,p) = 1 + \frac{\alpha\,(n-1)^{\beta}}{C(p)}, \quad
  C(p) = C_{\mathrm{base}} + (C_{\mathrm{max}} - C_{\mathrm{base}})\,p
  \label{eq:penalty}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $n$ is the total number of vehicles in proximity
(docked crew vehicles plus cargo vehicles actively unloading in that
period); $p = |\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{built}}| /
|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}|$ is the fractional build progress
at the start of the period; $\alpha = 0.1$ is the congestion
sensitivity parameter; $\beta = 1.5$ is the crowding exponent;
and $C(p)$ is the scale-adaptive site capacity.
The exponent $\beta > 1$ produces super-linear penalty growth:
adding a third vehicle when two are already present costs more
than adding the second, consistent with empirical coordination-overhead
models for confined workspaces.
The adaptive capacity $C(p)$ grows linearly from $C_{\mathrm{base}} = 2$
(sparse early-campaign structure with few docking nodes) to
$C_{\mathrm{max}} = 10$ (mature assembled truss with multiple
berthing ports and cleared approach corridors), reflecting the
physical reality that a more complete structure can absorb
simultaneous traffic with proportionally less interference.

The proximity penalty divides both EVA and robotic assembly hours:

\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm{EVA,eff}} = \frac{H_{\mathrm{EVA}}}{\pi(n,p)}, \quad
  H_{\mathrm{robotic,eff}} = \frac{H_{\mathrm{robotic}}}{\pi(n,p)}
\end{equation}

\noindent A penalty of $\pi = 1.5$, for example, reduces effective
crew hours by one third---equivalent to losing one EVA pair for
the entire period.
Because both crew and robotic operations are penalized equally,
the model captures the site-level disruption caused by simultaneous
approach, unloading, and assembly activities.

Cumulative collision risk is accumulated as a separate safety metric:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{R} = \sum_{t=0}^{T} \kappa \cdot \binom{n(t)}{2} \cdot \Delta t
  \label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\kappa$ is the baseline collision-probability
coefficient per vehicle-pair per day; $\binom{n(t)}{2}$ counts
the number of unique vehicle pairs in proximity at period $t$,
and the $\Delta t$ factor converts to probability per period.
The quadratic growth of $\binom{n}{2}$ with $n$ means that
simultaneous multi-vehicle operations are the dominant risk
contributor: two vehicles in proximity create one pair ($\mathcal{R}
\propto 1$), but four vehicles create six pairs ($\mathcal{R}
\propto 6$) for the same $\Delta t$.
The accumulated $\mathcal{R}$ is reported as a campaign-level
safety metric; it is not currently included in the objective $J$
but could be promoted to a fourth weighted objective in future
extensions.

\subsection{Partial Assembly (Work-in-Progress)}

Modules whose total assembly hours exceed the available capacity
in a single period are carried forward as work-in-progress (WIP).
Remaining hours $h_r$ are decremented each period by the minimum
of available capacity and outstanding need:

\begin{equation}
  h_r^{(t+1)} = \max\!\left(0,\;
    h_r^{(t)} - \min\!\left(h_r^{(t)},\; H_{\mathrm{available}}^{(t)}\right)
  \right)
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $h_r^{(t)}$ is the remaining assembly hours for
a WIP module at the start of period $t$; $H_{\mathrm{available}}^{(t)}$
is the effective crew or robotic capacity available to that module
after proximity penalties and other competing tasks are
deducted.
A module is marked complete and added to $\mathcal{M}$ when
$h_r \leq \varepsilon$ (default $\varepsilon = 0.01$\,h).
WIP tracking is essential for structurally demanding modules
such as habitat blocks (120\,h of assembly) or fusion reactors
(160\,h), which require two to four seven-day periods to complete
even with a full crew complement.
Without WIP, the model would force all assembly into discrete
all-or-nothing completions, artificially suppressing throughput
and misrepresenting the true campaign schedule.

\subsection{Vehicle Availability and Lead-Time Cost}

Each launch vehicle $v$ carries a \emph{lead time}
$\tau_v \geq 0$ (in periods) representing the minimum elapsed
campaign time before its first launch is permitted. This
captures procurement contracts, launch-site integration,
nuclear certification, and export licensing:

\begin{equation}
  t \;\geq\; \tau_v,
  \quad \forall\, v \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{cargo}} \cup
  \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{crew}}
  \label{eq:leadtime}
\end{equation}

\noindent The constraint is enforced as a hard gate: the vehicle
is simply not eligible for scheduling before $\tau_v$ periods
have elapsed, regardless of demand.
Vehicles are classified by readiness status:
\emph{Operational} vehicles (Falcon Heavy, SLS Block~2,
Vulcan Centaur, Ariane~6, GSLV Mk~III, H3) have
$\tau_v = 4$--26 periods (1--6 calendar months), covering
standard manifest integration and range safety reviews;
\emph{Near-term} vehicles (Starship, New Glenn, Starship HLS)
have $\tau_v = 13$--26 periods (3--6 months), reflecting ongoing
production qualification and regulatory certification;
and \emph{In-development} vehicles (KSLV-III) have
$\tau_v = 78$ periods ($\approx 18$ months), encompassing full
first-stage qualification, range approval, and export licensing.
Lead times therefore impose a structural lower bound on campaign
duration regardless of objective weights: no weight combination
can schedule the first KSLV-III flight before month~18.

Contracting, integration, and mission-assurance work incurred
during the lead-time window constitutes a one-time overhead.
This is captured as a \emph{first-flight cost premium}
$\delta_v \in [0,1]$ applied only to the first launch of
vehicle $v$:

\begin{equation}
  C_v^{(k)} =
  \begin{cases}
    c_v\bigl(1 + \delta_v\bigr) & k = 1 \\
    c_v                         & k > 1
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:firstflight}
\end{equation}

\noindent Here $c_v$ is the nominal per-launch catalog cost;
$k$ is the sequential launch index for vehicle $v$ across
the entire campaign; and $\delta_v$ is the fractional overhead.
Premium values range from $\delta_v = 0.08$ (8\%) for
operational vehicles with established integration procedures
(Falcon Heavy, Crew Dragon) to $\delta_v = 0.40$ (40\%) for
vehicles requiring full first-stage qualification testing
(KSLV-III), amortized over a single flight.
The premium discourages introducing new vehicle types late
in the campaign, where the one-time overhead cannot be amortized
across multiple flights, and reflects the contracting risk
associated with previously unused vehicle-launch-site combinations.
The total program cost $C$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:cost}) uses
$C_v^{(k)}$ in place of $c_v$ for all flights.

\subsection{Pad Turnaround Constraint}

Each launch vehicle $v$ is subject to a minimum inter-launch
interval to model launch pad refurbishment, range safety reset,
and vehicle re-manifesting:

\begin{equation}
  t - t_{\mathrm{last}}(v) \geq \Delta t_{\mathrm{pad}},
  \quad \forall\, v \in \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{cargo}} \cup
  \mathcal{V}_{\mathrm{crew}}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $t_{\mathrm{last}}(v)$ is the last period in which
vehicle $v$ was launched.
The default $\Delta t_{\mathrm{pad}} = 2$ periods (14~days) models
a modern rapid-turnaround expendable vehicle; reusable vehicles
with longer refurbishment cycles could be given higher values.
This constraint prevents the optimizer from scheduling the same
vehicle on back-to-back periods even when demand would otherwise
justify it, forcing vehicle diversity when assembly throughput
requirements are high and incentivizing the simultaneous use of
multiple vehicle types.

\subsection{Beam Search Pruning}
\label{sec:beam}

Exact DP over the assembled module bitmask is intractable for
structures with $|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}| \geq 20$
($2^{50}$ states for a 50-module spacecraft). Forward beam search
retains only the top-$K$ states per epoch. States are ranked by a
three-tier lexicographic sort key $\mathbf{k}(S)$:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{k}(S) = \bigl(
    -|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{built}}|,\;
    -\psi(S),\;
    J(S)
  \bigr)
  \label{eq:beamsort}
\end{equation}

\noindent The key is evaluated left to right; lower values are
better (the beam retains the $K$ states with the smallest keys).
$|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{built}}|$ is the number of modules fully
assembled in the current state; $\psi(S)$ is the blended secondary
score (Eq.~\ref{eq:psi}); and $J(S)$ is the current accumulated
weighted objective cost (Eq.~\ref{eq:cost}).

The secondary score $\psi(S)$ blends pipeline depth with the
accumulated objective cost:

\begin{equation}
  \psi(S) =
  \begin{cases}
    0
      & \text{if idle}(S) \\[4pt]
    \max\!\bigl(\,\phi(S)
               + 2\,|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}|
               - J(S)\cdot|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}|,\;1\bigr)
      & \text{otherwise}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}

\noindent A state is \emph{idle} if it has no cargo in transit
($\mathcal{Q} = \emptyset$), no modules at the site awaiting
assembly ($\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{built}}$), no
work-in-progress, and no crew vehicles docked
($\mathcal{C}(t) = \emptyset$); idle states score zero to prevent
the beam from filling with do-nothing trajectories when high cost
or launch-count weights make inaction appear cheap.
For active states, $\phi(S)$ measures pipeline depth
(Eq.~\ref{eq:phi}); the activity bonus
$2|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}|$ guarantees $\psi \geq 1$ for
every active state so active states always dominate idle ones;
and the cost penalty $J(S) \cdot |\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{total}}|$
scales the accumulated objective value to the same numerical range
as $\phi$, ensuring that high-cost trajectories are penalized in
tier~2 and not merely deferred to tier~3.

The pipeline-depth heuristic $\phi(S)$ is:

\begin{equation}
  \phi(S) = 3\,|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{built}}|
          + 2\,|\mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{built}}|
          + |\mathcal{Q}|
          + \sum_{v \in \mathcal{C}(t)} r_v
  \label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $|\mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{built}}|$
is the count of modules that have arrived at the assembly site but
not yet been fully assembled; $|\mathcal{Q}|$ is the number of
modules currently in transit; and $r_v$ is the number of rotation
periods remaining for crew vehicle $v$.
The integer weights 3, 2, 1 reflect decreasing certainty of
contribution: assembled modules are permanent; site-delivered
modules will be assembled within the next few periods if crew is
present; and in-transit modules will arrive in a future period
but may encounter WIP queuing delays before assembly can begin.
Crew coverage $r_v$ rewards states in which a freshly launched
vehicle will remain docked for many periods, distinguishing them
from states with an expiring crew and imminent assembly stall.

The three tiers serve distinct roles.
The \emph{primary tier} ($-|\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{built}}|$) is a
hard convergence guarantee: states that have assembled more modules
are never discarded in favour of cheaper but idle states,
ensuring the beam always makes forward progress toward campaign
completion.
The \emph{secondary tier} $\psi(S)$ has two responsibilities:
the activity bonus prevents the beam from filling with do-nothing
trajectories, and the cost penalty makes objective weights
effective even when pad-turnaround constraints force a near-term
scheduling choice between a cheap vehicle on cooldown and an
expensive vehicle that is currently eligible.
The \emph{tertiary tier} $J(S)$ resolves any remaining ties by
pure objective value, allowing the cost or launch-count sliders
to discriminate between otherwise equal schedules.
Default beam width: $K = 100$.

\paragraph{Weight sensitivity in practice.}
Weights produce visibly different solutions when the optimizer
faces genuine choices: multiple cargo vehicles with different
costs or lead times, or a spacecraft large enough to require
several launches.
With a single cargo vehicle selected, all weight combinations
converge to the same campaign architecture because the vehicle
mix cannot change---only launch timing varies.
The greedy beam structure also limits temporal trade-offs: a
trajectory that sacrifices early progress to wait for a cheaper
vehicle with a long lead time is penalized in tier~1 relative to
a trajectory that launches the immediately available vehicle; a
Pareto/NSGA-II search would preserve such trade-offs but is
computationally impractical for the state sizes considered here.
To explore cost-versus-time trade-offs, select at least two cargo
vehicles with different costs and compare runs on the Pareto
frontier view.
