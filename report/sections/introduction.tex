\section{Motivation}

The International Space Station (ISS) remains the largest and most
complex structure ever assembled in orbit. Its construction spanned
more than a decade, required over 40 dedicated launches across
multiple partner nations, and consumed more than 1{,}000~hours of
extravehicular activity (EVA) time across 160+ spacewalks to
connect, power, and commission individual
modules~\cite{nasa_iss_assembly,iss_legacy,mcdonnell_iss_eva}.  The
assembly campaign validated that orbital integration of
pressurized, power-generating, and life-support elements is
achievable, but it also exposed the fundamental cost drivers
of in-space assembly: launch cadence, crew rotation logistics,
and the coupling between simultaneous proximity operations and
productive build time.

As the civil and commercial space community plans structures that
eclipse the ISS in scale---kilometer-class solar power satellites,
large-aperture space telescopes, and crewed interplanetary
transfer vehicles---the assembly problem grows by more than an
order of magnitude in both mass and module count. At these scales
no single launch vehicle can deliver the complete system, and the
pattern demonstrated during ISS construction---a mixed fleet of
crewed missions to perform dexterous assembly and uncrewed cargo
flights to deliver mass---becomes a requirement rather than a
choice. Crew are essential for berthing, fluid connections, and
contingency response, while uncrewed tugs and heavy-lift cargo
vehicles deliver the bulk mass at a fraction of the per-kilogram
cost of a human-rated launch~\cite{wertz_larson}. Space-based
solar power concepts further amplify this requirement, with
baseline architectures calling for dozens of heavy-lift launches
per satellite~\cite{nsso_ssp}. The emergence of the Artemis
Accords framework~\cite{artemis_accords} also motivates planning
tools that accommodate a multi-national vehicle catalog.

This work targets the Earth--Sun~L4 triangular Lagrange point as
the assembly site. Unlike the collinear L1/L2 points, the
triangular libration points L4 and L5 are linearly stable for
mass ratios below Routh's critical value, which the Sun--Earth
system satisfies by a wide margin~\cite{l4_stability,
gomez_l4_dynamics}. Stability translates directly to minimal
stationkeeping propellant over decadal operations, in contrast
to halo orbits about L1 or L2 which require on the order of
several meters per second per year of maintenance
$\Delta v$~\cite{folta_l4_transfers}. The natural population of
Earth Trojans observed at L4~\cite{nasa_l4_survey} is empirical
evidence of this long-term stability. L4 also offers continuous
solar illumination with no eclipse seasons, supporting high solar
array output for construction-phase power; large angular
separation from the LEO debris belt that reduces shielding and
collision-avoidance overhead; and a quiet thermal and radiation
environment free from the geomagnetic trapped-particle
populations that complicate cislunar
operations~\cite{folta_l4_transfers}. These properties make L4 an
attractive staging location for long-lived structures that do
not need the close lunar access of a Gateway-style orbit. The
trade-off is a transfer $\Delta v$ from LEO of roughly
3.8--4.1~km/s and transit times of 60--120~days, which makes
resupply logistics a dominant contributor to life-cycle cost
and motivates the optimization framework developed in this
paper~\cite{belbruno_wbs,ho_logistics}.

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A dynamic programming formulation over a time-expanded
        network for assembly campaign optimization, with forward
        beam search to manage state-space complexity;
  \item A multi-objective cost function with user-adjustable
        weights over launches, time, and program cost, enabling
        Pareto frontier analysis;
  \item An interactive web-based simulation tool supporting
        parametric spacecraft configuration, real-time playback,
        and one-click PDF report generation.
\end{enumerate}
